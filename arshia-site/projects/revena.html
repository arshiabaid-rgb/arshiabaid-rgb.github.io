ReVena

A wearable brace that watches for the conditions that lead to deep vein thrombosis, like long immobility and weak peripheral blood flow, and responds with compression. It's built for everyday life, after hospital care ends.

01 · Why

Why I started

I watched my grandfather struggle with deep vein thrombosis. His intermittent pneumatic compression device was bulky, restrictive and kept him in bed. It was expensive and impractical, and it made me want a more accessible way to prevent clots.

DVT happens when slowed blood flow lets clots form in the deep veins of the legs. Globally, 8–10 million people develop it every year. In the US alone there are up to 900,000 cases a year and 100,000–300,000 deaths. Risk climbs after surgery, during cancer treatment, with heart disease, on long flights and at desk jobs. Yet monitoring usually stops at hospital discharge.

Today's options all leave a gap. Anticoagulants carry bleeding risks and need supervision. Hospital compression machines are tethered to a wall socket. The portable ones are "blind": they run on a fixed timer without checking whether the user is actually at risk.

02 · How it works

How it works

  1. Watching blood flow, not just oxygenAn optical PPG sensor on the leg tracks how strongly blood is reaching the tissue.
  2. A relative Oxygen Delivery IndexI calculated a relative Oxygen Delivery Index (rODI) for ReVena. Most wearables report oxygen saturation (SpO₂) alone, but blood can be well oxygenated and still not be reaching the limb, which is exactly what happens during stasis. rODI tracks changes in how much oxygenated blood actually arrives in the leg over time. It's a personal trend line, not a diagnosis, so ReVena can respond to a real drop instead of a fixed timer.
  3. Immobility from the calfAn accelerometer and gyroscope on the calf track movement. Long stillness (three hours in the design, with shorter test thresholds) is flagged as elevated risk, and small involuntary movements are ignored.
  4. Compression when it's neededWhen rODI drops or immobility runs long, ReVena alerts the user. A low-power pump inflates the cuff to 45–60 mmHg around the calf veins where DVT usually starts, and a solenoid valve then deflates it. Users can also trigger cycles from the app.
  5. Skin temperature (next)Cold skin narrows blood vessels and weakens the PPG signal without any real stasis. A temperature sensor will add that context so ReVena raises fewer false alerts.
03 · Who it's for

Who it's for

People recovering from surgery at home, cancer and heart patients, older adults, long-haul travellers and desk workers: anyone whose risk rises once they leave clinical care. The estimated cost to build one device is about $43.60, far below hospital compression systems.

04 · Status

Current status

  • Working: the ESP32-S3 circuit, stable power and Bluetooth LE, immobility detection on the calf, and pump and valve cycles tested for consistent, safe pressure.
  • In the app: device connection, alerts, and manual or automatic compression. Trend charts and the subscription features come next.
  • Still to do: testing the perfusion-index calculation on live PPG data, and adding the temperature sensor.
  • Protected: a patent application covering the core system design has been filed.

Physiotherapists, doctors and nutritionists have helped refine the compression settings and keep the goals realistic.

ReVena focuses on prevention, not diagnosis. It still needs clinical testing before anyone can say it reduces DVT risk.

05 · My role

My role

I lead ReVena's product development and strategy. I worked with Prof. Sachin Kumar at IIT Delhi on the mechanical design, using CAD in Fusion 360 and finite element analysis of how the cuff presses on a moving calf. I built the sensing and compression circuit and the app, and wrote the research paper behind it. Building KaamPro taught me to design for the people who will actually use a product, and I've tried to do the same here.
